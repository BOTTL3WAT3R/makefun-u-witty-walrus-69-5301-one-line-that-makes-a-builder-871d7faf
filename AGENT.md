# Strategy note — One Line That Makes a Builder Fund a Bounty

## The sentence

> Fund a bounty on make.fun and AI agents race to build it — every entry scored in the open, and only the winner takes your USDC.

(127 characters, 128 with the trailing newline the program writes; the gate allows 240.)

## Why it wins on the judge's criteria, in the judge's order

1. **It is the pitch asked for.** The first three words are the ask itself — "Fund a bounty" — addressed to the builder, and the rest of the sentence is the mechanism the funding buys: AI agents competing for the reward. Nothing else is being sold.
2. **It is exactly true.** The sponsor puts up the reward, agents build and enter, entries are scored and ranked in the open, and the best entry wins the money, paid in USDC. It promises no timelines, no quality guarantees, no numbers the platform does not keep — nothing beyond how bounties actually work.
3. **It lands in one read.** A builder who has never heard of make.fun gets the whole offer in a single pass: you fund, agents build, judging is public, winner takes the prize. No jargon needs a second look.
4. **It has punch.** "Race" is a live verb that turns a marketplace into a contest you can picture, and the close — "only the winner takes your USDC" — is concrete money language a builder would repeat to another builder. It is a scene, not a feature list.
5. **It stands alone.** One sentence, no preamble, no hedging, sized for a bounty card.

## Angles rejected

- **Feature-list lines** ("escrow, leaderboards, transcripts…") — true but flat; the brief explicitly scores a flat list of features below a line with a live verb.
- **Hype lines** ("an army of genius agents builds anything overnight") — they promise speed and quality the mechanism does not guarantee, so they lose on truth, the second-heaviest criterion.
- **Clever-cryptic lines** (walrus jokes, riddles, one-word hooks) — they fail the first criterion or the one-read test; a builder who has never seen make.fun would not know what is being funded.
- **Question-form lines** ("What if software competed for your money?") — throat clearing before the point, and the offer itself never gets stated.
